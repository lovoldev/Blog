What does the best technical blog look like? I often wonder about this.

With this question in mind, I've studied many blogs. On the whole, today's mainstream blogs can be divided into the following three categories:
1. Those that have their own viewpoint and, for the arguments made in the article, provide a process of description, analysis, solution, and so on. These blogs can be called fine works;
2. Those that have no viewpoint of their own, but, combined with personal practice, offer a few solutions. These blogs can be called ordinary works;
3. The worst are those with no viewpoint of their own and whose solutions are even wrong. These blogs can be called defective works.

Most of the blogs I've come across so far are primarily in a Q&A format: pose a question, then offer a way to solve it. The deeper ones will also give an analysis process. Admittedly, this is one approach, but I don't think it's the form a blog should take.

For me, a blog should be a very personal thing. While spreading knowledge, it should pay more attention to the expression of personality and the spread of ideas.
Science and technology are the primary productive force, but they are often only the foundation. Only when we build on the foundations inherited from our predecessors and make new innovations can new knowledge take shape and new productive forces emerge.
But innovation is not water without a source or a tree without roots. It requires a certain amount of knowledge (acquired through practice or theoretical study), on top of which one makes associations or analogies.
In this way, knowledge is passed down from generation to generation and accumulates more and more.

Since the way new knowledge is produced is through association and analogy, this tells us how we should learn new knowledge.

We should learn the skill of mastering knowledge, rather than mastering the knowledge itself.
The skill of mastering knowledge generally comes down to two basic methods, or two stages.
The first stage is called imitation.
Analogy means mapping new knowledge onto old knowledge — fitting the familiar onto the unfamiliar.
As a child I was very curious, but because my family was poor, the television was my only channel to new things — though for me back then, television was enough. At that time I loved two shows the most — one was *Art Attack* on the children's channel, and the other was *I Love Inventions* on the science and education channel. What attracted me most about these two shows was how wildly imaginative they were. *Art Attack* was a foreigner making small crafts with all kinds of tools or painting. I remember very clearly that once, through the television, he taught us how to draw cartoon characters using letters. That instantly touched my curiosity. I immediately got paper and a pen, noted down what he said and roughly what he did, then practiced. But no matter how hard I tried, I could never match the effect that foreigner achieved on screen. At the time I thought it was because I didn't have marker pens. Only much later did I realize I didn't have that talent. But none of that matters, because that show taught me a lot — how to think, how to turn waste into treasure.
*I Love Inventions* taught me another skill — practice. Although most of the inventions in that show were far too ambitious for an elementary school student, it still inspired me greatly. Because many of the inventors were farmers, some of whom had never even been to school and had farmed the land all their lives, yet purely on enthusiasm and hard study they still completed works that seemed impossible. I figured I'd already finished elementary school — I wouldn't say I was better than them, but at least I couldn't be much worse. So I went all out. I tinkered with homemade glue and a homemade motor boat. I took apart a TV and adjusted the big satellite dish. Of course, I gained no material achievements from these things, but I did gain spiritual satisfaction, trained my thinking ability, steeled my willpower, and improved my patience.
These things are undoubtedly valuable assets for my later studies, life, and work.

Back to the question of blogs. I think the various problems we encounter at work serve a role no different from those two TV shows — guiding us to think and to practice. Solving a problem is not the end point.
