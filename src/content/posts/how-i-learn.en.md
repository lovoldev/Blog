Since leaving school, I haven't studied anything new with single-minded focus. Every time I come across new knowledge, I go through excitement, indifference, disregard, forgetting. But the me from before wasn't like this.

Much of what I learned as a child I still haven't forgotten, and some things I did back then are still fresh in my memory. But the things I've been through since graduating seem as if they never happened — I can't recall them no matter how hard I try. I don't know where the problem lies. At first I thought I was just getting older and my memory was failing, but then how is it that I remember my childhood so clearly? Slowly I realized this isn't a memory problem, it's an attention problem.

As a child I was timid and afraid of strangers, so I spent most of my time playing by myself. And I wasn't a well-behaved kid either — I was always tinkering with things and could always find something to play with. Gradually I grew tired of the games I'd picked up from others, so I was in desperate need of a new way to burn my energy. Luckily, even though the family was struggling financially, my dad still bought a satellite receiver, and from then on I could finally watch TV during the day (though only on days when the rice was being cooked or when it rained without thunder). From then on, I felt I'd opened the door to a whole new world. Through the television I came across all sorts of new things and learned a great deal of useful and useless stuff. Back then I loved two shows the most — one was *Art Attack* on the children's channel, and the other was *I Love Inventions* on the science and education channel. What attracted me most to these two shows was how wildly imaginative they were. *Art Attack* was a foreigner making small crafts with all kinds of tools or teaching drawing. I remember very clearly that once he taught us how to draw cartoon characters using letters. That was so appealing — you should know that apart from these two shows, my favorite thing back then was cartoons of every kind, and I even dreamed of making my own animations when I grew up. So my curiosity was instantly sparked. I immediately got paper and a pen, noted down what he said and did, then practiced over and over on paper. But no matter how hard I tried, I could never match the effect that foreigner achieved on screen. At the time I thought it was because I didn't have marker pens. Only much later did I realize I simply didn't have that talent. But none of that matters, because I soon forgot about it — his imagination really was too big, with the ability to turn the mundane into something magical. His work often gave me that feeling of sudden enlightenment and sheer delight. Having watched a lot, I began to notice that sometimes new little ideas would pop into my head too, and that feeling was really wonderful.
*I Love Inventions* taught me another skill — practice. Although most of the inventions in that show were far too ambitious for an elementary school student, it still inspired me greatly. Because many of the inventors were farmers, some of whom had never even been to school and had farmed the land all their lives, yet purely on enthusiasm and hard study they still completed works that seemed impossible. I figured I'd already finished elementary school — I wouldn't say I was better than them, but at least I couldn't be much worse. So I went all out. I tinkered with homemade glue and a homemade motor boat. I took apart a TV and adjusted the big satellite dish. Of course, I gained no material achievements from these things, but I did gain spiritual satisfaction, trained my thinking ability, steeled my willpower, and improved my patience.
These experiences were all the motivation and source for my later learning.

Now, sitting in front of the computer and recalling these past events, they still get my blood pumping. I haven't lost these abilities, but they've been abandoned by me for far, far too long.

After graduating I studied languages like Python and Go one after another, and also relearned the basics of computer science. But I didn't achieve any clear breakthrough in any of them — I was missing that feeling of sudden enlightenment and that giddy excitement.
